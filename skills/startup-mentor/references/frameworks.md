# Frameworks

Original paraphrased notes on startups, inspired by Paul Graham's free public essays. Use as background; answer in your own words and never quote.

## Signature numbers (show one line of math)

| Situation | Number → verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Weekly growth (revenue or active users) | 5–7%/week good · 10% exceptional · ~1% = not figured out yet |
| Compounding | 1%/week ≈ 1.7×/year; 5%/week ≈ 12.6×/year |
| Weekly target, concretely | 100 users → 10 new next week = 10% growth |
| Default alive test | Constant expenses + current revenue growth: profitable before cash runs out? If not, default dead; fix now |
| Runway pinch | ~6 months runway, heavy burn, flat growth = fatal pinch; with ≤8 months, raise now rather than trusting "promised" follow-on |
| Ramen profitable | Revenue ≥ founders' living expenses (e.g. ~$3k/month for two frugal founders) = unlimited time, better investor leverage |

## Ideas

- **Notice, don't brainstorm.** The best ideas come from problems the founders have themselves; brainstormed ideas tend to sound plausible and be wanted by nobody. Being at the leading edge of a field lets you see what's missing.
- **Well vs. wide.** Better a small group who want it urgently than a large group who want it mildly. Start with the deep well, then widen.
- **Sitcom ideas.** Ideas that sound good to everyone and that nobody actually needs (the classic: social network for pet owners). Test: who wants this so badly they'd use a crappy v1 from an unknown company?
- **Schlep blindness.** Founders unconsciously avoid ideas involving tedious, unglamorous work (payments, regulation, sales), which leaves those ideas open.
- **Seems like a bad idea, but is good.** The most valuable ideas look wrong or toy-like at first; if it were obviously good, someone would be doing it.
- **Frighteningly ambitious ideas** (replace search, replace email, new way to make software) are worth approaching sideways, starting small.
- **Make the company more powerful.** Ask how it could own the customer relationship, have money flow through it, become a platform others build on, or gain network effects. Generalizing the idea often reveals a marketplace.
- **Competitors** rarely kill startups; worry more about building something nobody wants.

Questions: Did you have this problem yourself? Who wants it most urgently, by name? What would make it more powerful? What schlep are you avoiding?

## Users and product

- **Make something people want.** The one thing that matters most; most failures are founders building something not enough people want.
- **Do things that don't scale.** Recruit users by hand, one at a time. Give early users an absurdly good experience. Do the work manually before automating.
- **Launch fast, iterate.** A v1 with a narrow purpose shipped early beats a polished launch; the real work starts with users.
- **Talk to users.** Watch what they do, not what they say they'll do.

Questions: How many active users? What did your last ten users say? What do you do by hand that you could do more of?

## Growth

- **Startup = growth.** A startup is a company designed to grow fast; growth is the compass for nearly every decision. Pick one metric (ideally revenue or active users) and track the weekly rate. 5–7% a week is good, 10% exceptional, 1% means you haven't figured it out.
- **Growth from users who love it** compounds through word of mouth; that is how founders get rich without doing anything bad.
- **The top idea in your mind** should be the product, not fundraising or disputes.

Questions: What's your weekly growth rate on your key metric? What drove the last good week?

## Runway and survival

- **Default alive or default dead?** Assuming current expenses and current growth, do you reach profitability on the money you have? Founders often don't know; calculate it. If dead, fix growth or cut costs now rather than counting on fundraising.
- **The fatal pinch.** Default dead + slow growth + assuming you'll raise. Investors notice slow growth too.
- **Ramen profitable.** Make just enough to pay founders' living expenses; it changes your relationship with investors and buys unlimited time.
- **Don't die.** Startups rarely die from competition; they die when founders give up. Staying alive buys luck time to work.
- **Spend frugally.** Hiring too fast and fancy offices are classic killers.

Questions: Monthly burn? Months of runway? Monthly growth? Are you default alive?

## Fundraising

- **Raise when you don't need to, with momentum.** Investors are herd animals: interest snowballs and evaporates.
- **Fundraising is a distraction**: get it done fast, in parallel, and get back to work. Have a clear "how much" and keep the process breadth-first.
- **Convince investors** by being formidable and showing why this could be huge: the truth, told clearly.
- **High-resolution fundraising**: close investors one at a time on rolling terms (often SAFEs) instead of a single round.
- **Equity equation**: giving up a fraction x is worth it only if it makes the rest worth more than 1/(1−x) times as much (selling 20% needs the company to end up 1.25× more valuable).
- **Corp dev meetings are acquisition processes.** Don't take them unless you want to sell.

Questions: Do you need the money, or want it? What's your growth? How much, for what milestone?

## Cofounders and team

- **Have a cofounder**, and pick one you know and trust; fights between founders are a leading killer.
- **What makes a founder**: determination over intelligence; relentlessly resourceful; flexible on ideas but stubborn on the goal.
- **Hire slowly**; early hires are costly and the founders should do the early work.
- **Founder mode**: founders scaling up should stay engaged in details instead of fully delegating by the conventional manager playbook.
- **Mean people fail**; being nice is safe for founders.

## Founder psychology and focus

- **What startups are really like**: harder, longer, and more emotional than expected; cofounders and users matter most.
- **Maker's schedule**: protect long blocks; meetings cost makers half a day.
- **Good procrastination**: neglect errands, not the important work.
- **Earnestness and taste**: care about the thing itself, not the image.

## Deciding to start

- **Startups are counterintuitive**: you can't game them like a class; the best preparation is building things.
- **Most reasons to wait are weaker than they sound** (too young, no business experience, no idea yet). Test each one against a specific missing fact.
- **Location**: go where the startup people are.

## Competitors

- **Competition is a sign of a good idea.** You can only avoid competitors by avoiding good ideas; err toward spaces where you'll face them.
- **Startups usually die by suicide, not murder**: worry about whether users want it before worrying about rivals.
- **Superlinear returns**: a product half as good as the rival's doesn't get half the customers, it gets almost none, so win on one dimension decisively.
- **Incumbents miss what looks like a toy.** Large companies dismiss small, odd beginnings; that is your opening.

Questions: What do your users switch from? On which one dimension are you dramatically better?

## Pitching and interviews

- **Let the startup do the work**: understand why it's worth investing in, then explain that plainly. Investors want formidable founders, a big possible outcome, and evidence; slickness counts for little.
- **Interviews are not pitches.** Investor and accelerator interviews probe whether you understand your users and are building something; be clear, concrete, and brief.
- **One-sentence test**: if you can't say what you do in a sentence, investors and users can't either.
- **Write it out.** Writing the pitch exposes holes in the thinking.

## Hiring

- **Don't hire too early or too fast.** Hiring before product/market fit burns money and slows you down; founders should do the early work and stay small.
- **Great people care about interesting problems and good tools**, and productivity varies enormously between them.
- **Startups beat jobs for the ambitious**: a small company's acquisition is how big companies buy proven talent now.

Questions: What would this hire do that a founder can't? Are you growing fast enough that hiring is the bottleneck?

## Wealth and ambition

- **Wealth is what people want**, not money; startups compress a working life into a few years by making something valuable.
- **Superlinear returns** come from exponential growth and thresholds; aim at work whose returns compound, and start early.
- **Great work**: pick something you have aptitude for and deep interest in, get to the frontier, notice gaps, work hard, stay curious.
- **Ambitious ideas are frightening**; approach them sideways from a small starting point.
- **Early work looks lame.** Push through the embarrassing stage.

## Taste and making

- **Good taste exists** and can be learned; good design is simple, timeless, solves the right problem, and is often slightly funny.
- **Make new things**: find a powerful simple idea, build for yourself, ship rough, iterate.
- **Copy what you like**, not what you're supposed to like.
- **Brand vs. substance**: when a product becomes a commodity, brand fills the gap; prefer winning on substance.

## Writing and thinking

- **Write simply**, in spoken language; it raises engagement and exposes weak ideas.
- **Writing is thinking.** Putting ideas into words reveals what you don't know.
- **Think for yourself**: novel and correct ideas require independent-mindedness; the best insights are general and surprising.
- **Update in a changing world**: test beliefs that depend on the world against it; bet on people with new ideas.
- **Keep your identity small** so you can think about contested questions clearly.

## Life and career choices (non-startup questions)

- **Life is short**: cut the nonsense, do what matters now, savor time with people you love.
- **Do what you love, with judgment**: weigh interest against money and certainty; it depends on the trade.
- **A project of one's own** produces the best work and motivation.
- **What you'll wish you'd known**: aim for options and work on hard problems while young.

## Office-hours questions

The questions to reach for first:
1. What's your growth rate, week over week?
2. Who wants this most, and how do you know?
3. Are you default alive?
4. What are you doing by hand that you could do more of?
5. What would make this company more powerful?
6. What's the top idea in your mind when you wake up?
