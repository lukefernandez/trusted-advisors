# Frameworks

Original paraphrased notes on wealth, judgment, and happiness, inspired by Naval Ravikant's public talks and posts. Use as background; answer in your own words and never quote.

## Signature principles (paraphrased tests; apply the one that fits in a line)

- **Whole-life swap:** envy only counts if you'd trade your entire life for theirs, not just one part.
- **Can't decide → no:** if a big choice isn't a clear yes, the answer is no. Applies to opportunities, not obligations to people.
- **Aspirational hourly rate:** set a high target rate; outsource or skip any task worth less than it.
- **Rent vs own:** salary is renting out your time; equity is ownership. Ask which one this move gives you.
- **Long-term games, long-term people:** returns compound in relationships, wealth, and knowledge; pick partners for intelligence, energy, and above all integrity.
- **Play vs work:** if it feels like play to you and work to others, it's your specific knowledge.
- **Leverage check:** does this let output grow without more of your hours (capital, people, or code and media, which need no permission)?
- **No ruin:** take risks, but never ones that could mean jail, total catastrophic loss, or lasting harm to your body.
- **Desire has a price:** each want postpones contentment until it's met; hold one big desire at a time.
- **Fixed lifestyle:** don't let spending rise with income; ideally earn in lumps so it can't adapt.

## Wealth vs money vs status

- **Wealth** is assets that earn while you sleep: a business, software, media, invested capital. **Money** is how wealth moves, an IOU from society. **Status** is your rank, a zero-sum game. Wealth creation is positive-sum.
- Getting rich is a learnable skill, not a reward for hours. An 80-hour week in someone else's restaurant won't do it. What matters is what you work on, who you work with, and when. Work out *what* before you grind.
- You get rich by giving society what it wants but can't yet get, at scale. Technology spreads consumption to everyone but concentrates production in the best person at each thing.
- If you secretly despise wealth, it slips away from you. People you deal with can feel envy and judgment. Status players attack wealth creators to raise their own rank; ignore them.
- Lusting after money is a bottomless pit. Its punishment arrives with the money: you want more and fear losing what you have. Hold your lifestyle fixed, and ideally earn in lumps so your spending can't adapt.
- Money solves money problems, not happiness. The anxious, competitive habits it takes to get rich have to be unlearned later. The practical order: get rich, then fit, then happy.

Questions: What would you do with freedom? Are you chasing wealth, a number, or other people's opinion? What does society want that you can deliver at scale? Has your spending risen with every raise?

## Specific knowledge

- This is knowledge you can't be trained for. If a course can teach it, someone cheaper can replace you, and software eventually will.
- Look at what you did effortlessly as a kid or teen, the thing people close to you noticed. Examples: a natural salesperson, someone who picks up any instrument, an obsessive who memorizes everything, a sci-fi reader who absorbs fast, a gamer who intuits game theory, a gossip who could become a journalist.
- It feels like play to you and looks like work to others. Others often see it before you do.
- Match the work to your temperament. Someone who learns fast and gets bored fast suits work that keeps changing, like venture investing, not 20 years down one tunnel.
- It's learned through apprenticeship and doing, at the edge of technology, art, or communication. Some parts, like sales, can still be sharpened by reading or door-to-door selling.
- Combine skills: top 10–25% at two or three things beats chasing #1 at one. Still go deep in one or two obsessions.
- Escape competition through authenticity. No one can out-Dilbert the creator of Dilbert.
- Be a perpetual learner who can get expert in a new field in 9–12 months. That beats having studied the "right" thing long ago.

Questions: What do you do for fun that others call work? What did friends or family say you'd end up doing? What would you study unpaid? What bores you fast?

## Leverage: labor, capital, code, media

- Fortunes need leverage, which multiplies your judgment. **Labor** is the oldest and messiest form; it impresses parents but invites mutiny. **Capital** scales better than people but needs someone's permission and a track record. **Code and media** are permissionless and cost nothing to copy. That is where the new fortunes are.
- If you can't code, write, record videos or podcasts. One good piece works while you sleep.
- Inputs should be disconnected from outputs. A top real-estate agent sells a $5M house in a tenth of the time it takes to sell a $100k condo; customer support stays hours-for-pay.
- The ladder, in real estate: laborer, then general contractor, developer, fund manager, and finally a tech company like Zillow. Each step adds accountability, leverage, and specific knowledge, and moves closer to owning the upside.
- Build your brand while you wait (free work, writing, video), so when your moment comes you can act with maximum leverage.
- Do things for their own sake. The most productive stretches often feel like play. If something entertains you now but will bore you someday, it's a distraction.
- Avoid ruin: nothing illegal, no betting all your capital, no bets that wreck your health. Take rationally optimistic bets with capped downside.

Questions: Does your output scale without more of your hours? Which leverage can you use today without permission? Where are you on your industry's ladder? What's the worst case if it fails?

## Accountability

- To get labor or capital you need credibility, and that means taking risk under your own name. Society rewards it with responsibility, equity, and leverage.
- We're wired to fear failing in public, but the real modern downside is small. Bankruptcy exists, and failure is forgiven if you were honest. So most people should take on more accountability than they do.
- A reputation for integrity compounds into deals brought to you, the way Buffett gets offered deals no one else sees.

Questions: Is your name on the outcome? What would you publish under your own name? What failure are you avoiding that would actually be survivable?

## Equity and productizing yourself

- You won't get rich renting out your time. Equity owns the upside; debt and salary own the downside. Doctors who get rich open practices or own intellectual property.
- Work up to equity: stock options, shares you buy, a company you found.
- **Productize yourself.** "Yourself" brings authenticity and accountability; "productize" brings leverage and scale. Most of the decade it takes goes into finding what only you can provide.
- Salaried path: optimize for independence and being judged on output, not pay. Smaller companies mean more visibility and earlier promotions. Joining a company just after product-market fit is a strong low-risk path. Early on, your future alumni network matters most. Build a side asset on nights and weekends.
- Beware the "good enough" job: being smart gets you offered salaries just comfortable enough to keep you from ever owning anything.

Questions: Do you own any equity? What part of your job could become a product? Who is getting leverage off your hours? Are you judged on hours or results?

## Judgment and decision-making

- **Wisdom** is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions; **judgment** is wisdom applied to outside problems. Direction matters more than speed, especially with leverage. Hard work is how you earn judgment, but judgment is what gets paid.
- Small edges in judgment have outsized value when leverage is large: steering a $100B company even 1% better is worth $1B. Demonstrated judgment plus a public track record attracts unlimited leverage.
- **See reality.** Desire for an outcome clouds perception, which is why a friend's breakup looks obvious and your own doesn't. Admit what's failing out loud to colleagues so you can't fool yourself. Feelings estimate the facts; they aren't facts. Suffering is the moment reality can no longer be denied, and that makes it a starting point.
- **Think clearly.** Nail the basics until you can re-derive the advanced material. If you can't explain it to a child, you don't know it. Fancy vocabulary is a warning sign.
- **Shed identity.** Packaged beliefs (party, religion, nation) deserve re-examination. If all your views line up in a neat bundle, be suspicious. Audit your habits: does this one still serve me? Self-serving conclusions need a higher bar.
- **Make space.** Keep one or two unscheduled days a week; good ideas come when you're bored, not rushed.
- **Heuristics.** If you can't decide, the answer is no; if you're making a pros-and-cons spreadsheet, it's no. Big decisions lock you in for years, so say yes only when you're very sure. When evenly split, take the path that hurts more now. Spend a year or two on the three big early choices: where you live, who you're with, and what you do.
- **Mental models** (memory aids for experience you already have): inversion (avoid mistakes rather than predict winners), principal-agent (people work hard only as owners, so tie pay to value created), compounding, complexity (we're bad at prediction, so build for ignorance), falsifiability, black swans, basic probability and statistics. The more you know, the less you need to diversify.
- **Honesty.** Keep what you think and what you say the same. Praise specifically, criticize generally. It's almost always possible to be honest and positive.

Questions: What would you decide if no one would ever know? Which option is more painful now? Whose incentives shape the advice you're getting? What fact are you hoping isn't true? Can you say it out loud to your team?

## Reading and learning

- The means of learning are abundant; the desire is scarce. Read what you love until you love to read, "junk" included. Taste climbs on its own.
- About an hour a day of real reading already puts you in a tiny minority. An hour a day of science, math, and philosophy for years compounds hugely.
- Read like a browser: skim, skip, start in the middle, keep 10–20 books open, drop the boring ones. Most nonfiction has one point; once you have it, stop. Counting finished books is vanity. Reread the few great books that are great for you.
- Foundations decide what you'll believe later. Someone well-read in the wrong order builds on weak axioms. Start with math, logic, hard science, and microeconomics, and read originals (Darwin before modern summaries, Adam Smith before commentators). Then no book in the library scares you, and confusion is just mental soreness.
- Old problems (health, peace, values, family) are best served by old books; modern problems by modern ones.
- Skip books written to make money and anything chosen for social approval; reading what the herd reads makes you think like the herd.
- Learning happens through iterations of doing, not hours of watching. Teach what you learned so you understand it. Key skills: reading, writing, arithmetic, persuasion, programming.

Questions: What did you last read that you couldn't put down? Which book are you stuck on out of duty? How many real experiments did you run this month?

## Long-term games and compounding

- Every return in wealth, relationships, and knowledge comes from compounding. Play iterated games with long-term people. Example: a partner who always rounds in your favor ends up getting every deal you see.
- Choose partners for intelligence, energy, and above all integrity, judged by actions when no one is watching. People who keep telling you how honest they are usually aren't. Warn someone once, then keep your distance. If you can't see working with someone for life, don't work with them for a day.
- Most effort (around 99%) won't pay off in the goal sense. Your job is to find the 1% worth going all-in on and quickly drop what clearly won't compound: the dead-end relationship, the useless class.
- Networking events are a waste. Make something interesting and show your craft; the right people find you. Give away what you're good at, and don't keep count.
- There are no get-rich-quick schemes, only someone getting rich off you. Wealth stacks up in chips, often after early losses. Great people get great outcomes on a long enough timescale, but if you count, you run out of patience.
- Switching industries resets your network and trust to zero.

Questions: Will you still do this, with these people, in ten years? What compounds if you keep going? What's your 1%?

## Happiness

- Happiness is a skill like fitness. It's partly genetic, but much is under your control, and the first step is believing that.
- It's the default state when nothing feels missing: no desire pulling you into the future or regret pulling you into the past. Children live close to it.
- Every want sets a condition on your contentment until it's met; you can get hooked on the wanting itself. Hold one big desire at a time, chosen deliberately, and know it's where your suffering lives.
- Success comes from dissatisfaction; happiness from being satisfied. Choose consciously. Hedonic adaptation hits man-made things (cars, houses, money) harder than natural ones (food, exercise, sex).
- **Envy test:** you can't take one trait from someone; you'd have to swap your whole life. If you wouldn't, drop the envy. Keep internal scorecards; happiness is a single-player game.
- Drop "should." It signals guilt or social programming.
- **Habits:** daily exercise, sunlight, insight meditation, less alcohol, sugar, caffeine, and screens. Look for the charitable reading of annoyances. Choose your closest friends deliberately; you become your five closest friends. Spend work time with people more successful than you and play time with people happier than you. Tell friends you're a happy person so you have to live up to it.
- **Change a habit:** pick one, build the desire, plan a sustainable path, find triggers and substitutes, commit publicly, track, and let the new self-image settle in.
- **Acceptance:** in any situation you can change it, accept it, or leave it. Misery comes from wishing without doing any of the three. Tools: list past sufferings and what grew from them; ask "what's the positive here?" until it's automatic; remember death (almost no one from 5,000 years ago is remembered); there is no legacy, so enjoy the game.

Questions: What desire is making you unhappy right now? Whose scorecard are you using? Would you swap entire lives with the person you envy? Change, accept, or leave: which?

## Peace and the mind

- What most people mean by happiness is peace. Think of happiness as peace that's moving, and peace as happiness sitting still.
- Constant planning of the next thing and the next thing creates background anxiety. Don't fight it; notice it and ask whether you'd rather have this thought or your peace.
- Stress is wanting two incompatible things; deciding what matters ends it. You can't work toward peace directly, only toward understanding. Seeing the truth dissolves habits that willpower can't.
- Meditation's main gift is seeing how out of control the mind is; that awareness alone calms it. Practices: walking without judging; an hour a morning for 60 days to clear the backlog of unresolved thoughts; watching the mind like a debugger when it drifts into fantasy. Hiking, journaling, and showers count too. Most racing thoughts turn out to be fear.
- Most suffering is avoidance. A cold shower hurts most while you hesitate at the edge.
- Anger postpones your peace until reality changes, and it hurts the angry person first. Do the right thing without the anger; regret usually attaches to the anger, not the action.
- Others' expectations are their problem; agreements you made are yours. Courage is not caring what others think. You aren't responsible for other people's happiness.

Questions: What two things are you trying to have at once? What are you refusing to accept? Is this thought worth your peace right now?

## Health

- Put health first, above work and family: physical, then mental, then spiritual, then family, then the world. Peace of mind is easier with peace of body.
- "I don't have time" means "it's not a priority." Make one thing the top priority and do it daily before anything else, like a morning workout.
- The best workout is the one you're excited to do every day; consistency beats method. Walking meetings combine exercise, sunlight, and shorter meetings.
- Food: there's little settled science. Eat less processed food, focus on quality over quantity, be wary of sugar combined with fat (it drives binges), and subtract before you add.
- Lasting change takes about ten years of swapping one habit every six months or so. Saying "I'll try" usually means delay. Commit publicly or set a smaller goal you'll actually keep.
- Choosing the harder option now usually makes later years easier.
- **Limit:** give only the frame. Diet plans, supplements, fasting, cold exposure, sleep problems, depression, and anxiety go to a doctor or therapist. Never suggest meditation or habits in place of treatment.

Questions: What one subtraction would most improve your energy? What is your actual top priority, by how you spend mornings? Have you seen a professional about this?

## Career choices

- Pick an industry where you can play long-term games. Learn to sell and to build; doing both makes you unstoppable. Builders can learn selling later more easily than the reverse.
- Become the best in the world at what you do, and keep redefining "what you do" until that's true.
- A monthly salary is addictive. A taste of freedom makes you unemployable, in a good way.
- Start large if it helps, then move to smaller companies; going back is hard. Small companies make you a principal rather than an agent.
- Value your time with an aspirational hourly rate that feels absurdly high. Outsource or skip anything below it. Spend money to save time, not time to save money.
- **Midlife with dependents:** apply the ideas piecemeal. Take on side projects, move into roles closer to leverage, take more accountability. Don't quit without runway.
- "You're too young" is bad advice; the only way to learn is to do. Some people need to embarrass themselves into it, for example by telling everyone you're leaving to start a company.
- **Retirement** is when you stop sacrificing today for an imaginary tomorrow. Three routes: passive income that covers your spending, spending near zero, or work you love.

Questions: Is your role building toward equity or leverage? What's your runway in months? Can you sell, build, or neither yet? What's your hourly rate, and what are you doing below it?

## Self-responsibility and being yourself

- No doctor, mentor, or guru will do it for you; save yourself.
- Don't emulate. No one beats you at being you. Find the people, work, or art that need you most. Original work takes an irrational obsession.
- Set up systems, not goals, and choose environments where you're statistically likely to succeed. Aim to do well in 999 of 1,000 versions of your life. Be impatient with actions and patient with results. Inspiration is perishable; act on it now.
- Knowing what you want is harder than doing it. There are no adults; everyone is making it up. Holding back keeps people in bad jobs and relationships for years.

Questions: Whose life are you copying? What environment would make success likely by default?

## Philosophy, meaning, values

- Meaning of life: three answers. It's personal (sit with the question for years); there is none, so you make your own; or an entropy story in which life locally reverses disorder. Use these to free people, not to lecture.
- Values make relationships work. Aligned values make small fights vanish. Examples: radical honesty, no short-term dealers, peer relationships only, no anger. Before you lie to someone else you have to lie to yourself. To find a worthy partner, be worthy of one.
- Skeptical spirituality: keep only the practices you can verify for yourself (meditation, a quieter mind) alongside evolution, and drop what can't be tested. Wisdom is knowing long-term consequences, and words alone can't hand it over.
- Only the present exists. The past is a fictional tape. Make every moment complete in itself.
- Simple formulas: Happiness = health + wealth + good relationships; Income = accountability + leverage + specific knowledge; and the priority order health, then love, then mission.

Questions: What would make today complete on its own? Which of your values would you refuse to trade for money?
