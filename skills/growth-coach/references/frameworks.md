# Frameworks

Original paraphrased notes on offers, pricing, leads, sales, and retention, inspired by Alex Hormozi's free public teaching. Use as background; answer in your own words and never quote. All numbers are rules of thumb; the user's own data wins.

## Find the constraint first

- Every stuck business is limited by one of four things: not enough leads, leads that don't buy, customers who don't stay or get results, or too little profit per customer. Name the one; fixing it often makes the next three goals moot. Don't start three fixes at once.
- Test: if leads doubled tomorrow, could sales and delivery handle it? If yes, it's leads. If leads have the problem and the money but don't buy, it's sales or the offer, not the ads.
- Simpler businesses (one product, one buyer type, one channel) scale further. Think in orders of magnitude, not increments.

Questions: Leads per month, close rate, monthly churn, gross margin? Where does work pile up?

## Pick the right market

- Market beats offer beats persuasion. A buyer group in urgent pain, with money, that's easy to reach and growing will buy even from a mediocre seller; a great product sold into a shrinking market still dies.
- If a market fails one of those four tests, expect to need unusual skill to compensate.
- Narrow until the message feels written for one person. The same advice sold to a specific job title can command 10–100× the generic price.
- Describe their problem more precisely than they can and they'll assume you can fix it.
- Commit to the niche once picked. Most owners who think a niche has capped them need to be better, not broader. Try several offers in a good market before blaming the market.

Questions: Who has this problem worst and can pay? Where do they gather? Is the market growing, flat, or shrinking?

## What makes an offer worth more

- Perceived value rises with how big the outcome is and how sure the buyer is of getting it, and falls with how long it takes and how much work it asks of them. Two products promising the same result are priced by certainty, speed, and effort, not by the promise.
- Bigger promises are easy and cheap to copy. The durable edge is cutting time and effort: done-for-you beats done-with-you beats do-it-yourself, and prices accordingly. People pay to skip the line, which is how you compete with free.
- Certainty comes from proof, track record, and guarantees. Engineer a real win in the first 7 days; the long-term result is what they buy, early wins are why they stay.
- Fixing how a wait *feels* can beat fixing the wait (a progress tracker, a scheduled check-in).
- A strong claim names the outcome, the timeframe, and the pain they avoid.

Questions: What's the outcome in their words? What proof do they see before paying? What's the first win and how fast? What work could you take off their plate?

## Building the offer

- Walk the customer's path before, during, and after buying, and list every reason they'd fail or quit (too hard, too slow, won't work for me, not worth it). Expect 30–60. Turn each into a solution, then pick the cheapest way to deliver it: 1:1, group, or self-serve; live or recorded; done for them or with them.
- Cut items that cost a lot and add little first, then cheap items that add little. Keep build-once assets (templates, calculators, checklists) that serve every client at near-zero cost.
- One unsolved objection loses the sale. Be flexible about *how* you solve it.
- Response rate, close rate, and price multiply: 2.5× response × 2.3× close × 4× price ≈ 23× cash per marketing dollar. Small gains on all three beat a big gain on one.
- Easy-to-sell offers are usually hard to deliver. Over-deliver early to learn every obstacle, then systematize to cut delivery cost.
- A bonus that solves a problem that only exists after they succeed signals you expect them to succeed.

Questions: Top five reasons customers fail or quit? Which does the offer solve? What could you build once that serves every client?

## Pricing

- Price is the biggest single lever: it buys margin, better service, and ad budget. Most small operators are underpriced, and being the second-cheapest has no strategic value.
- Price to the outcome, not hours or features. Offer a discount for paying up front.
- Close rate as a pricing signal (salesperson-led sales only; rough guide): if almost everyone qualified buys (80%+), test a much higher price, 2× or more. Around 50–70%, test 20–50% higher. Around 30–40%, you're likely near the right price if buyers arrive informed. Under about 30%, fix lead quality or the sales process before cutting price.
- At full capacity: raise price first, then serve more people per hour (1:1 to small group), then add capacity.
- Price add-ons and higher tiers well above the core, often several times, because a minority of buyers spend most of the money.
- Raise prices on existing customers with notice and an honest reason; grandfather openly, never through secret deals.

Questions: Close rate at this price? Gross margin? How many clients before quality drops?

## Honest urgency, scarcity, bonuses, guarantees

- These change how the offer is presented, not what it is. Use only constraints that are real.
- **Scarcity:** publish real capacity (clients per month) and how full you are; limited runs and small premium tiers work when genuinely limited.
- **Urgency:** cohort start dates, seasonal promotions with real end dates, bonuses that truly expire, announced price increases.
- Never invent the constraint: no resetting countdowns, no fake "spots left." It's lying, erodes trust, and can break consumer-protection law.
- **Bonuses beat discounts.** Discounting the core teaches buyers your price is negotiable. Each bonus should remove one specific objection; tools and checklists beat more training. Give honest values and disclose any partner commissions.
- **Guarantees** move risk off the buyer: unconditional refund; conditional (do the agreed actions and if it doesn't work, refund or keep working free), which lifts both sales and completion; all sales final with a stated reason; or pay-for-performance. Never guarantee health or financial outcomes you can't control.
- **Naming:** a good offer name says who it's for (local beats generic), the result, a timeframe where allowed, and the format (challenge, sprint, intensive). When response fades, refresh the name and creative before changing the offer.

Questions: What's genuinely limited? Which objection does each bonus remove? What would you guarantee if they do the work? Is every claim provable?

## Getting leads

- There are only four ways to reach people: contact people who know you, one at a time; publish to people who know you; contact strangers one at a time; pay to show strangers ads. Not enough leads means not enough volume or skill on one of these.
- Start with your existing contacts (it works today), then publishing, then cold outreach and ads.
- Volume floor: about 100 actions a day (messages, minutes of content, or ad dollars) on one channel for 100 days before judging it. Better: work until you hit a set number of conversations, not hours.
- Order of growth: first do more of what works, then fix the biggest drop-off with one test per platform per week, then add new channels.
- One small test proves nothing: 300 flyers vs a pro's 5,000 a day. Mass media often responds at roughly 0.5–1%.
- Keep about 1–10% of ad budget for experiments with no expected return.

Questions: Which of the four are you doing, at what daily volume? Where do leads drop off? What's this week's test?

### Publishing content

- Every piece must earn attention in the first seconds, keep it, and deliver what the opening promised. Headlines need a reason: relevance, recency, conflict, the unusual, a known name, or an ongoing story.
- Topics: lessons from the past, what you did this week, documenting now, reacting to news in your niche, or running an experiment to create a story. Client stories and your own mistakes are the best raw material.
- Give far more than you ask; ask in private, give in public. Track content to revenue, not views.

### Cold outreach and ads

- Cold outreach adds three problems: getting a list (interest groups are the warmest cold leads), saying something worth hearing (personal, simple, value first, ask for interest in a free resource before a sale), and following up (more times, more channels; retry non-responders after 3–6 months). Aim for roughly 3% of a cold list becoming engaged leads.
- A cold-outreach rep pays when their leads produce at least ~3× their cost in lifetime gross profit; it rarely works for cheap consumer offers.
- Ads: say who it's for, the value in their words, and one action with a real reason to act now. Set up tracking of cash per ad first.
- Ad testing budget: let a new ad spend up to ~2× the 30-day gross profit of one customer; kill it at ~1× with no leads. Budget backward: customers wanted × CAC, plus ~20% for efficiency loss at scale.
- Content that performs organically usually makes a good ad.
- Follow opt-out, spam, and telemarketing rules (CAN-SPAM, TCPA, FTC) and disclose paid relationships.

### Referrals, staff, and partners

- Beyond your own effort, leads come from customers (referrals), employees, agencies, and partners with audiences full of your buyers. Referrals are the cheapest and best leads; they come from results, so ask at the moment of a win and make it easy.
- Hire for a channel with a written checklist; measure payroll ÷ engaged leads as CAC. Use agencies to learn a channel or fill a gap, then bring it in-house.
- Partners: list who already reaches your buyers, give them a simple way to earn, have them invest something to qualify, and keep them promoting. Ten new partners a month compounds; ten new customers a month doesn't.

## Free offers that generate leads

- A free resource should fully solve one narrow problem and, in doing so, reveal the next problem your paid offer solves. If the paid offer sells directly, skip it.
- Three kinds: reveal a problem (audit, diagnostic), a sample or trial, or one step of a multi-step job (calculator, template). Deliver as software, information, service, or a physical item.
- Make it as good as your paid work; give away the what, sell the doing. End with one clear call to action.

Questions: What's the first problem your buyer hits? What could they use in 10 minutes? What does it reveal that you sell?

## Sales calls

- A simple call structure: learn why they're here, name the problem back to them, ask what they've tried, sell the result (not the process), handle concerns, then reassure them after they buy.
- Restate an objection before answering it. Most objections are stalls about time, money, another decision-maker, or fear.
- Process beats talent: call new leads within about a minute; confirmations and reminders lift show rates; use a written checklist before hiring star closers.
- "Too expensive" usually means not enough value or the wrong lead. Fix value and qualification first; offer a payment plan or a smaller entry option, not a lower price for the same thing.
- Keep sales calendars about 70% booked: higher loses leads, lower means too many reps.

Questions: Close rate and show rate? Share of objections about price? Minutes to first call?

## Sequencing offers for cash

- Plan the order of what you sell: a front-end offer that gets customers, an upgrade right after, a lighter option for those who say no, and a recurring offer. Add one at a time and make each reliable before the next.
- Good: 30-day gross profit from a customer exceeds the cost to get and serve them. Great: it covers two or more, so cash stops limiting growth.
- The front-end offer often makes little; the upgrades make the money. Each offer solves a problem and reveals the next; offer the fix when they notice it.
- Front-end options: free or cheap entry with a premium version shown side by side; pay up front and earn it back as credit by doing the actions that produce results; buy more, pay less per unit; pay now at a discount or later with a conditional guarantee. A refund rate above ~10% means the promise is too big, conditions too loose, or price too high.
- Upgrades: price the next offer around 5× current spend; roughly 1 in 5 buyers will take it. Present it the moment it's needed, recommend one option, and credit past spend toward a bigger commitment. A real warranty or guarantee at 5–50% more is a valid upgrade.
- For those who say no: never sell the same thing cheaper. Change how they pay (plan, financing, split payments) or what they get (fewer components). Ask commitment on 1–10: 8+ means keep offering payment options; 7 or below means change the offer.
- Recurring: offer it within the first 30 days. A bonus for joining, free time for a longer term, or a setup fee waived for a longer commitment all work.
- Pricing standalone vs membership (rough guide): standalone at ~1.3× its membership equivalent gets about half choosing membership; ~2× gets most.
- Every fee, penalty, and condition disclosed up front in plain words; cancelling as easy as signing up; check auto-renewal, giveaway, and financing rules with a lawyer.

Questions: What do you sell first, second, third? 30-day gross profit vs CAC? What do people who say no buy instead?

## Retention

- Revenue grows only when joins exceed cancels. Track both monthly.
- Churn usually spikes in month 1, then around months 3 and 6. Fix the first 30 days first.
- Onboarding is re-selling: restate their goal, deliver a quick win, give written next steps and a named contact.
- Ceiling = new customers per month ÷ monthly churn. 30 new/month at 10% churn caps at about 300.
- Longer billing terms usually churn less than month-to-month (subscriptions only; check your cohorts). Never trap people with hard cancels.

Questions: Monthly churn? When do people leave? What happens in their first 7 days?

## Unit economics: CAC, LTV, payback

- Lifetime gross profit = gross profit per period × periods retained. Use gross profit, not revenue. Aim for at least 3:1 against CAC; early ratios are often higher and fall with scale.
- Example: $1,500/month at 50% margin for 4 months = $3,000 gross profit; at $2,400 CAC that's 1.25:1. Fix retention or margin before spending more.
- Payback target: recover CAC within about 30 days. Longer works with capital but caps growth. If 30-day gross profit is under CAC, you fund growth from savings or debt; at 2×+ CAC, customers fund the next customers.
- CAC within ~3× your industry's typical figure is workable; above that, it's an ad or sales problem.

Questions: CAC by channel? Gross margin? Months retained? Cash collected in 30 days?

## Fit notes

- **Enterprise B2B:** value and proof transfer; volume tactics don't. Use account-based outreach, multiple stakeholders, pilots with defined success criteria, and ROI cases per stakeholder.
- **Regulated (health, finance, legal):** no outcome guarantees on bodies or money; claims need substantiation; route copy through compliance.
- **Service businesses scaling:** own locations, franchising (costly, litigious), licensing (cheap, high margin), or tech-enabled service.
- **Pre-revenue:** contact people you know daily to land the first 5 customers; sell before building.
- **Solo creators:** cut effort rather than add hours: raise price, productize, add a recurring tier, use partners.
