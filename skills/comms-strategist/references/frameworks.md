# Frameworks

Original paraphrased notes on comms, PR, and going direct, inspired by Lulu Cheng Meservey's free public writing. Use as background; answer in your own words and never quote.

## Signature principles (paraphrased tests; apply the one that fits in a line)

- **Business ends only:** a comms goal (views, hits, "go viral") is not a goal; if you can't draw a straight line to customers, hires, retention, or capital, it's wasted motion.
- **Solve the formula backwards:** objective → action needed → who must act → what they must believe → only then the tactic.
- **Loud isn't audience:** cater to the people who decide your outcome, not the angriest or flashiest voices.
- **Assume on the record:** nothing is off the record until both sides agreed before you spoke.
- **Headline in the first answer:** pick one message and land it immediately; segments get cut.
- **Details beat descriptions:** one vivid fact or number outweighs ten adjectives, for you or against you.
- **Say "I'm sorry I…":** a named human, the specific thing, the real impact, only airtight promises, then follow through unprompted.
- **Credibility thermostat:** critics push until they feel heard; out-own them and they pull back. Rebutting escalates.
- **Flip the frame, address your people:** correct a bad story by speaking to your audience, not the outlet, from a position of strength.
- **Own the elephant:** say the awkward fact (down round, layoff, leak) yourself before critics can.
- **Stay on offense:** maximize stories you tell versus stories told about you; announce it yourself, since it'll leak.
- **Real beats loud:** in a sea of slop, real craft, real evidence, and real events stand out; narratives take 6–18 months.

## Strategy

- Comms exists to make the business more valuable. Ends are business goals (customers, engineers, a round, regained trust), max three. Ways are tactics. Means are budget, hires, agency; be realistic about ROI.
- A good plan executed now beats a perfect plan in a doc. Most comms fails on execution, not insight. The whole strategy fits one page.

Questions: What are your top three business goals? What will you actually spend (time, money, people)?

## Tactics

- Formula: plan X instills belief A in audience B so they take action C, achieving objective D. Solve D, C, B, A first; X is then a bounded choice. One formula per objective; tactics that recur across formulas are your best bets.
- Audience is where companies fail: name the people who matter, what they care about (message), where they get information (medium); tune out the rest. Catering to non-audience causes mission drift.
- Belief A takes empathy and testing; tone-deaf comms means a wrong guess at A.
- Channel follows audience: Instagram over TV for young city dwellers; local radio can out-earn the biggest national outlet; niche podcasts and forums over TIME for developers.
- Build trust with third-party advocates, credible reports, unpaid customer stories (more believable than paid), founder outreach 1:1.
- Bold beats safe: avoiding risk yields errors of omission, worst for a startup fighting the status quo. Rule out illegal, unethical, obviously dumb; then make educated bets and learn from contact with reality.

Questions: Who exactly must act? What must they believe that they don't now? Where do they get information?

## Going direct and founder voice

- Reach people through your own channels instead of relying on intermediaries with misaligned interests. Going direct ≠ going alone, and ≠ just posting more; it means owning your story.
- Comms is the founder's job: the story is the product; outsourcing it is like outsourcing code. Delegate execution (inbound, outreach), never the narrative.
- People trust people: be the human mascot. Your genuine traits (shy, awkward, funny) beat polish. Speak as a person; show real emotion; get angry if falsely accused.
- Spotlight several distinct executives so one stumble doesn't jail the brand; make spokespeople non-interchangeable.
- Natural medium: use the format that shows your strength (video if you're good on camera, writing if you think on the page). Mix formats: blog for control, press for reach, short video for engagement, audio for nuance.
- Educate more than you sell: an honest broker explaining the industry earns trust the shill can't.
- Pick your peer group: appear beside credible people; skip low-value crowded panels. A look only works if authentic.
- Talk when you have no news, or people assume trouble whenever you appear.

Questions: What can you say that no one else can? What medium are you naturally good in? Who are your second and third voices?

## Standing out and attention

- Everything is becoming fake; the edge is real things: craft with taste, evidence of outcomes, physical events and artifacts, real humans with flaws, durable relationships. These may not maximize clicks; quality beats virality.
- Real narratives take 6–18 months of consistency.
- Positioning: decide luxury (scarcity, insiders, authenticity, influencers) or mass-market (practical benefits). Never make a new product look like a knockoff of an old status symbol. Message what it does for the customer, not for your revenue.
- Long-form shows: pick for format and fair host, not reach alone; skip scripted talking points; judge by business metrics afterward (signups, new customers).

Questions: What real proof can you show? Is this a status product or a practical one?

## Crisis playbook

- Stay ready: before any crisis, keep a war-room contact list (offline copy), assign roles (lead voice, comms, legal, people), and draft holding statements for top scenarios (breach, product failure, financial collapse, disaster).
- Is it a crisis? Injury or death, illegal behavior, broken core promise, lasting reputation harm, government involvement, material financial hit.
- Priorities: people's safety, accuracy (be the single source of truth), transparency only about what's confirmed, speed, focus, legal counsel on everything.
- Break-glass: assemble by phone; tell employees directly and early; route all press to one person; brief key stakeholders and third-party allies; pause tone-deaf campaigns.
- Message: what happened, what you're doing, where to get updates, what they must do, the path forward.
- Spokesperson: never lie or speculate; no "no comment" (say you don't know and how you'll find out); calm, declarative; no humor or sarcasm; correct falsehoods fast before they harden; reframe unfair premises; assume everything is on the record.
- Bad press reveals rather than creates problems; a sound company survives it. Internal critics hurt most.

Questions (ask first): What actually happened, and how sure are you? Who was harmed? Who knows? Any legal or regulatory duty to disclose?

## Apologies

- A named person says "I'm sorry I…" (not "if," not "you," not "we regret"). Name the specific wrong, no passive voice; explanation, not excuse.
- Describe their impact so they don't have to; never "at least." Go all in rather than nibbling.
- Amends: promise only what's airtight and literally true. Then deliver early or beyond, and follow up unprompted.
- Pick a lane: a full apology or none; no half-apology with jabs.

Questions: What exactly did you do wrong? Who was affected and how? What can you guarantee?

## Negative feedback

- Reputations work like a thermostat; critics push until it feels right. Separate the frustration from the information; resolve the frustration first.
- Rebuttal escalates and onlookers side with the customer. Align on principles (quality matters, feedback is welcome, you own it), take more responsibility than seems necessary, explain rather than defend. Bad-faith trolls are different: ignore or fight.

Questions: Is this a real customer or a troll? Is any of it true?

## Correcting headlines

- Speak to your audience, not the outlet. Flip the frame to strength; gratitude reads more confident than rebuttal. Separate a fair reporter from a misleading headline. Don't insult the people the label describes. Choose the format that shows the truth (video of reality beats a written denial).
- If the headline is true, this is an apology, not a correction.

Questions: What's literally wrong in it? Who saw it that matters to you?

## On / off the record

- Default is on the record, including "no comment." Agree terms before you speak, in plain English (named? described how? usable?).
- Off record: unusable, but they can confirm elsewhere, and it still shapes their view; never share a real secret. Background: usable without your name; negotiate the description. Skip deep background.
- Any conversation, DM, or dinner can become an interview; it's not over until you're out of earshot. Don't toggle mid-interview. Don't go on background for benign facts.

## TV, podcasts, profiles

- TV only if it fits the audience and you're good live. One headline message, landed in the first answer; stats and stories, not adjectives. Brief the producer (context, intro bullets, visuals) and ask for the chyron. Watch the show's tape. End answers with a period; never memorize. Thank host and producer; reuse clips.
- Profiles: go/no-go on objectives, news cycle, readiness, reporter fit, backup plan, overexposure. Time it to a strong moment; limit access (no gym scenes); cap reporting at roughly 2–6 months; expect other voices; agree ground rules in writing; walk away if it's going wrong.

## PR agencies

- Early: do it yourself. Then hire a great full-time comms lead. Agencies help with outreach, inbound, launches, crises; keep the story and writing in-house.
- Choose via founder referrals; meet the actual day-to-day team; proposal speed is the fastest they'll ever be; check that they know the players personally.
- Fees: retainers from about $5K/month (freelancer) to $500K+ (global firm); typical tech firm $20–35K; specialist $60–100K. Avoid hourly. Start with 3–6 months.
- Manage to business goals, not share-of-voice; meet daily in crisis, weekly for day-to-day, monthly for strategy; read their pitches.

Questions: What can't you do yourself right now? Do you have a comms lead?

## Fandom and virality

- Remove barriers: kill jargon, publish across formats, stay out of politics unrelated to your mission, take strong stands on your mission.
- Spotlight individuals; foster community and identity (names, inside jokes, meetups, strategic swag, ideally wearable by dogs or babies); bring people into the process (build in public, shared milestones).
- Memes: give a good canvas (absurd, visual, emotional, debatable, personal); make content a vehicle for people to express themselves; offer a tribe to join and a foil to rally against. Luck can be engineered, not guaranteed.

## Newsletters and solo audience

- Value proposition: what you offer, why you uniquely, for whom. Niche first. Self-promote without apology.
- Metric: growth of the free list. One great post beats ten mediocre. Weekly cadence if you can.
- Gateways: show up where your audience is; target adjacent topics; collaborate and comment; use recommendations and guest posts. Spike on launch, news hooks (fast beats perfect), and moments you create.

## Fundraising announcements

- Five ingredients: objectives (energize employees, recruit, reassure and win customers), audience (not the general public), message (customers, progress, plans, not the money), execution (own blog and founder thread first, then on-record press), validation (customers, employees, investors vouching).
- Own awkward facts yourself (down round). If it leaks, don't rush; reset on your own timeline.

## Recruiting comms

- Money is necessary but not sufficient. Sell inevitability (winners join winners), a mission no other company could claim, glory and autonomy, esprit de corps, stars you celebrate by name, freedom from bureaucracy, a leader worth following, and visible momentum.
- New hires are your best recruiters; hire fast. A sharp name or brand attracts fits and repels misfits.

## Persuasion psychology (light use)

- Familiarity and proximity build affinity; small favors and reciprocity build it more; minor flaws make a competent person more likable. Use only honestly.
