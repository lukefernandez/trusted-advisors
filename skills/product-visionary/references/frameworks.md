# Frameworks

Original paraphrased notes on product focus, taste, and teams, inspired by Steve Jobs's public talks and interviews. Use as background; answer in your own words and never quote.

## Signature principles and numbers (apply the one that fits in a line)

- **Focus is no:** focus means refusing good ideas, not picking the obvious one. Count what you're doing; you can do about three things great.
- **17 → 4:** on returning, the product line went from about seventeen products to four (consumer/pro × desktop/portable). Test: can your whole lineup fit a 2×2 a customer understands in ten seconds?
- **Customer first, tech last:** start from the experience you want the customer to have and work back to the technology; never start with a cool technology and hunt for a market.
- **Sum greater than parts:** many locally sensible projects that don't add up are worse than fewer that compound. Ask: does this make the whole better?
- **Design is how it works:** not the veneer; the way the thing behaves for the person using it. If you can't explain how it works simply, it isn't designed yet.
- **Simple is harder:** simplicity comes after you understand the problem deeply; the first solutions are complex, the great ones come out the other side.
- **Idea ≠ product:** believing a great idea is 90% of the work is a disease; the craft of turning it into a product is most of the work, and the idea changes as the team builds it .
- **100:1:** in software the best person can be ~100× better than the average (vs ~2:1 in most fields), so a small team of A-players beats a large team of B-players. Spend a big share of founder time (roughly a day a week) on recruiting.
- **A's attract A's:** get about five A-players together and they love working with each other and won't tolerate B and C work; don't settle.
- **Fewer people, even if doing less:** headcount past a certain line turns doers into managers and hurts the product.
- **Hire people to tell you what to do:** the best hires decide; ideas win on merit, not rank.
- **Marketing is values:** in a noisy world people remember one or two things; say what you stand for, not specs.
- **Choose what you love carefully:** you only have so much love/attention to put in; spend it on few things.

## Focus and saying no

- Before cutting, cluster requests into the few underlying customer problems and weight each by revenue at stake and churn risk; then pick the top problems and say no to the rest.
- Kill good projects, not just bad ones. Saying no upsets people (staff, fans, press); do it anyway, and explain the new whole.
- Prune the lineup until each product has an obvious customer. If you need a chart to explain which to buy, there are too many.
- A roadmap is a list of things you won't do this year.

Questions: If you could ship only one thing this quarter, what is it? What does each item add to the whole? Which customer would actually miss this if it vanished?

## Customer-first

- Begin with the experience: what the person feels, does, and gets. Then pick the technology that delivers it. Demo the result (the printout, not the controller in the box).
- Customers can't always say what they want until they see it; that's not license to ignore them, it's a duty to understand them better than they can articulate.

Questions: Describe the moment the customer gets value, in one sentence, no tech words. What do they use today?

## Simplicity

- Remove until it breaks, then add back one thing. Every option is a decision you're pushing onto the user.
- Defaults matter more than settings. Hide nothing important; delete what's unimportant.
- Simplicity in the product often means complexity you absorb in engineering.
- Keep only steps that are legally or technically required; replace setup with defaults or sample data so value shows first.
- Verify every simplification with a measurable outcome: A/B the shorter flow on activation, task time, or day-7 retention.

Questions: Which settings do 90% of users never touch? What's the one task a first-time user must succeed at?

## Design as how it works

- Design starts at the core of the product and works outward; it is not a coat of paint at the end. Designers and engineers work together from the start.
- Judge design by using it, not by screenshots.

Questions: Who owns the experience end to end? When did a designer last sit with an engineer before code?

## End-to-end experience

- Own (or tightly control) every touchpoint that shapes the experience: product, onboarding, packaging, support, billing, store. Seams between parts are where products feel cheap.
- Integration wins when the experience depends on it; not every company must build everything, so own the parts customers feel.

Questions: Map every step from hearing about you to the tenth use: which step embarrasses you?

## Craft and quality

- Most companies rot when leaders lose the feel for a good versus a bad product and the craftsmanship it takes; keep that feel at the top.
- Care shows in details customers never consciously notice. Polish, but ship: results are what shipped.
- Quality starts with people; there are no shortcuts.

Questions: What part would you be embarrassed for a customer to inspect? What's the ship date and the one thing it must nail?

## Product taste

- Taste comes from exposure to the best things humans have made, outside your field (typography, art, architecture), then bringing them into your work. Connect dots from varied experiences.
- Taste is choosing: knowing what to leave out.

Questions: What are three products you love and why? What did you cut last week?

## Launches and demos

- Tell one story: the problem, the one-line reason this is different, then show it working live. Three reveals max. One memorable line.
- Announce when it ships or nearly; don't sell vapor.
- Rehearse the live demo end to end and keep a fallback (second unit, recorded clip) so one failure can't sink the story.

Questions: What's the one sentence you want people to repeat? What's the demo moment?

## Marketing and values

- Be clear on the one thing you want people to know. Brand is what you stand for; celebrate your customers, not your specs.

Questions: What do you believe that competitors don't? Who is your customer as a person?

## Team and A-players

- Small team of A-players; hire slow, fire bozos fast. Judge experienced hires by results, juniors by how they think and how much they care. Push in interviews to see who argues back with substance.
- Recruiting is the founder's job.

Questions: How many people are on the core team? Who on it is not an A, and what are you doing about it?

## Managing and candor

- High standards stated plainly: say when work isn't good enough, specifically, about the work, not the person. Great people want honest feedback; they don't want managers, they want a vision and room.
- Ideas win by merit; debate hard, then commit.
- Never abuse, threaten, or humiliate; that drives away the A-players you need and creates legal risk. The candid version is specific, private when personal, and paired with what great looks like.

Questions: What exactly is wrong with the work? Have you shown what great looks like?

## Competitors and copying

- Don't copy; study why the best thing works, then make yours better and different. Copying traps you a step behind and invites IP trouble (trade dress, patents, copyrighted assets).
- Learn from great work broadly and bring in ideas from outside your field; borrow principles, never pixels, assets, or trade dress.
- When declining a copy, give the usable path: open-licensed assets (e.g., Lucide or Phosphor icons, an open font), your own palette and tokens, and a first design task to start today.

Questions: What does the competitor get right for the customer? What do you believe they get wrong?

## Career and calling

- Do work you love; keep looking until you find it. Detours make sense only in hindsight, so a winding path isn't a wrong one. Use mortality as a filter for what matters.

Questions: If this were your last year, would you be doing this?
